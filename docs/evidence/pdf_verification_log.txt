Behavioral Activation Protocol for Persistent Mild De-
pression and Lethargy
Introduction
Behavioral Activation (BA) is a well-established, evidence-based treatment that addresses the cycle of 
depression by increasing engagement in meaningful activities. When we're depressed, we tend to withdraw 
from activities that once brought us joy or a sense of accomplishment, which then reinforces feelings of 
sadness, worthlessness, and low energy. This creates a downward spiral where inactivity feeds depression, 
and depression fuels further inactivity.
This protocol is designed for individuals experiencing persistent mild depression and lethargy—those who 
may feel consistently low-energy, unmotivated, or disconnected from activities they previously enjoyed. 
Rather than waiting for motivation to return (which often doesn't happen without action), Behavioral Activation 
focuses on taking small, manageable steps to gradually rebuild positive routines and experiences. The goal 
isn't to eliminate difficult feelings immediately, but to create opportunities for positive reinforcement and a 
greater sense of control over your daily life.
Goals
• Increase weekly engagement in activities rated as moderately pleasant or meaningful (target: 3-5 
activities per day)
• Reduce daily time spent in passive or avoidant behaviors (e.g., excessive screen time, staying in bed) 
by 25% within two weeks
• Improve self-reported mood scores by 20% over four weeks, as measured by a simple daily mood 
tracking scale
Steps
Step 1: Activity Monitoring and Awareness
Begin by tracking your current activity patterns and mood for 3–5 days before making any changes. Use a 
simple hourly log to record:
• What you did during each hour (be specific—include work, meals, rest, scrolling, etc.)
• Your mood rating on a 0–10 scale (0 = worst mood, 10 = best mood)
• Whether the activity was something you chose or felt obligated to do

This step builds awareness of how your behaviors and emotions interact throughout the day. Many people 
discover that certain activities correlate with better moods—even small ones like making the bed or stepping 
outside—and that avoidance behaviors often maintain low mood without providing relief.
Step 2: Schedule Meaningful Activities
Using your activity log, identify 1–2 activities per day that align with one of these categories:
• Mastery Activities: Tasks that give a sense of accomplishment (e.g., organizing a drawer, cooking a 
meal)
• Pleasure Activities: Things that bring enjoyment or relaxation (e.g., listening to music, walking in nature)
• Social Connection: Interactions that foster connection (e.g., texting a friend, joining a group)
• Routine Maintenance: Basic self-care tasks (e.g., showering, eating regular meals)
Start small. For example:
• Monday: Take a 10-minute walk after lunch + call one friend
• Tuesday: Cook breakfast instead of skipping it + listen to favorite playlist while cleaning
Write these into your calendar or planner as non-negotiable appointments with yourself. If an activity feels too 
overwhelming, break it down further (e.g., "walk" becomes "put on shoes and stand outside for 5 minutes").
The rationale here is that depression narrows our behavioral repertoire—we stop doing things that could 
improve our mood simply because we don't feel like it. By scheduling activities in advance, we bypass the 
need for motivation and create structure that supports gradual improvement.
Step 3: Monitor and Adjust Based on Feedback
Each evening, briefly review your day using this framework:
• Which scheduled activities did you complete?
• How did your mood change before vs. after each activity?
• What got in the way of completing activities?
• What would you adjust tomorrow?
If an activity consistently leads to increased energy or improved mood, try expanding it slightly next week 
(e.g., increase walk duration from 10 to 15 minutes). If something feels draining or unhelpful, modify or replace 
it.
This reflective process helps reinforce learning and prevents discouragement. Progress isn't always linear, 
and adjustments are part of the process—not signs of failure.
Practice
Commit to the following daily routine for at least two weeks:

Morning (within first hour of waking):
• Complete a brief morning routine (shower, get dressed, make bed)
• Schedule 1–2 specific activities into your day (written down or in calendar)
Throughout the day:
• Check in with your mood twice daily (mid-morning and mid-afternoon) using a 0–10 scale
• Engage in at least one mastery or pleasure activity
• Limit passive screen time when possible (set timers if needed)
Evening (last 10 minutes before bed):
• Reflect on the day using the questions above
• Plan tomorrow's key activities
Use a paper journal, app, or printed worksheet to track your progress. Consistency matters more than 
intensity—small actions done regularly will compound over time.
Reflection
At the end of each week, spend five minutes answering these questions:
• What activities consistently lifted your mood, even slightly?
• What patterns emerged in your energy levels throughout the day?
• When did you feel most like yourself this week—and what were you doing?
• What barriers kept you from engaging fully, and how might you address them next week?
• How has your relationship with planning or choosing activities shifted since starting this protocol?
Notice whether your sense of agency—the feeling that you can influence your own experience—is growing 
stronger. Even minor shifts in behavior can lead to meaningful improvements in outlook over time.
Conclusion
You've taken an important step by engaging with Behavioral Activation. Remember, the goal isn't perfection 
but progress. Some days will feel easier than others, and that's completely normal. Be patient with yourself 
as you rebuild routines and reconnect with sources of meaning and joy.
If at any point your symptoms worsen significantly, interfere with basic functioning, or include thoughts of 
self-harm, please reach out to a mental health professional right away. While this protocol can be helpful for 
mild depression, it is not a substitute for individualized care when needed. You deserve support, and help is 
always available.
